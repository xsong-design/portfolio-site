Liquidity was one of the big bets the Marketing Messages team had for the year. For us it meant integrating marketing messages into ads creation.

Marketing messages are campaigns sent to subscribers — people who have already shown interest and opted in. The business can then reach them directly with a coupon, a seasonal promotion, a Black Friday offer. Ads reach people who have not made that commitment yet. The two do not do the same job.

<StrategyPair />

Ads already lived in Ads Manager. Marketing messages followed a similar creation pattern, but sat in a different part of the product. Advertisers did not want two budgets, and they were reluctant to leave a workflow they knew for a channel whose benefit was not obvious.

We wanted marketing messages inside ads creation in order to leverage Meta’s optimizations — one of the strongest capabilities in the ads ecosystem. We already had optimizations for ads and for marketing messages. We did not have them operating as one system. That is Liquidity.

I was one of the senior designers brought on with a Design Lead to define the end-to-end, from onboarding through reporting. That took a few sprints, with advertiser testing and reviews with Ads and Marketing Messages leadership along the way.

## Budget comprehension

Coming out of those sprints, leadership reviews, and tests, the budget strategy was set: rely on optimizations, and keep advertiser control of the split as limited as possible. A manual mix would defeat the purpose of the system. Over time, those optimizations — using ads delivery data and the rest of what Meta already knows — should get better at choosing what is best for the business.

I led budget comprehension because that model was not landing. Advertisers are highly sensitive to spend. They need to know where money is going, and that optimizations are working across both channels rather than favoring one.

In prototype tests, ten performance marketers in Brazil, India, Mexico, Indonesia, the US, and the UK walked the integrated flow. Nine of ten expected every subscriber to receive a message, often with marketing messages taking budget first. Uploading a list felt like a guaranteed send. The list is the eligible pool. Optimizations use it, with the budget, to decide the mix. Full delivery is not guaranteed.

> “With subscribers, they are my loyal customers so I expect significantly higher conversion and expect bulk of my budget (60–70%) to be assigned to WhatsApp. I will not be OK with the reverse because only I can determine the potential with WhatsApp for my business.”

Marketing messages have to be enabled on the ad account through onboarding. The person who does that is often not the person who later creates the ads, so the change is easy to miss.

### What we shipped

<FigureSlot title="Shipped — L2 right-hand rail and inline budget guidance" caption="Hero for this section: the Ads Manager screens that shipped" />

Ads Manager has three levels: L3 campaign, L2 ad set, L1 ad. Budget for a mixed campaign is set at L2 — that is also where a set can include both ads and marketing messages — so that is where we shipped.

<CampaignLevels />

Two pieces of guidance: a sticky card in the right-hand rail, and an inline message on the budget field. The rail tells the person in creation that the account now includes marketing messages, and what that changes, including budget. The inline message is there if they never read the rail: this budget covers marketing messages as well as ads.

### What we explored

We explored giving advertisers control of the split. Leadership aligned on optimizations only. We looked at onboarding at L3; campaign setup is the wrong altitude, because the fields that change sit at L2. On L2 we considered a global banner, then chose the sticky, expandable rail plus a message on the budget field itself.

## Reporting

Reporting is horizontal. Any change in that table meant working with both the metrics team and the ads team, and we had little control over the reporting design itself. I was asked to lead it because the work is collaborative, and collaboration and communication are where I am strongest.

The metrics were not new. What was new was the strategy: marketing messages had never been added to ads reporting this way, and that required onboarding. It was a first for Marketing Messages. Most advertisers were not on Liquidity, so those columns did not belong in the default table. They did belong for anyone who had sent a mixed campaign.

I joined the ads team’s sprint on new experiences. Together we defined when the new-user experience appears, how it is written, and what happens after they answer. If they agree, the table updates to include two marketing-message metrics. If they decline, we point them to controls that already existed — turning metrics on later, or customizing the campaign reporting table. The work was making those options clear, not inventing a new way to rearrange the table.

## Takeaways

What I would keep is sitting in the ads team’s sprints. Reporting only works if the NUX is their table, their language, and a path that already existed — not a Messaging overlay. It was faster to learn that in the room than from decks.

What I would change is bringing GTM into the budget conversation as soon as testing showed people expected a guaranteed send. A lot of that education has to come from a rep, not only from the rail. I would also document decisions more tightly. Leadership from different orgs was reviewing a fast sprint, and it was hard to track what had actually been agreed.

The larger lesson is that Liquidity fails if people do not trust spend. Optimizations can allocate across ads and messages; they cannot, on their own, explain where the money went. Budget comprehension and a reporting NUX for a new strategy are the same problem at two moments in Ads Manager.

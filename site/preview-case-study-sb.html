Redesigning Search
at Walmart Scale.

I designed the foundational search modules — Top Picks and Shop By — that transformed Walmart's overwhelming search results into guided, contextual shopping experiences, reducing actions-to-cart by 70% and lifting conversion rates across tested queries.

My Role Product Designer, Search & Browse
Team 2 UX Designers, PMs, Data Scientists, Researcher
Timeline April 2019 – Q1 2020
Platform Walmart.com + Mobile App
Company Walmart Labs — Ecommerce
Modular search experience — Top Picks and Shop By modules integrated into search results, adapting to product category and customer intent Hero · Product Screenshot
01

Context & Challenge

The Business Problem

Walmart's e-commerce assortment was growing rapidly, but the search experience hadn't kept pace. A query like "AA batteries" returned over 6,266 results. Rugs — the #1 search term on Walmart.com — surfaced 130,000+ items. Customers were overwhelmed, and the data showed it: 47% of all tracked online customer feedback related to search and browse.

Customers couldn't find what they wanted, felt confused and lost in pages of results, and the existing sort and filter tools weren't helping — customers were tapping roughly 20 actions before adding a single item to cart.

Before State The existing search experience — a wall of undifferentiated product tiles with no guidance or contextual filtering. "AA batteries" returns 6,266 results.

What Made This Hard

Search is deceptively complex because shopping journeys vary dramatically. Sometimes customers know exactly what they want and just need the cheapest option fast. Other times they're researching high-consideration items like TVs, comparing specs they may not fully understand. The product category itself changes the journey — batteries are low-consideration, rugs are high-consideration, and Samsung TVs sit somewhere in between with brand affinity and technical complexity layered on top.

Designing a single search experience that adapts to all of these contexts — across an inventory of millions of products — required a fundamentally different approach. We needed a modular system, not a monolithic redesign.

02

Discovery & Strategy

How I Built Understanding

I joined Walmart in April 2019 and spent the initial 4-5 months deeply embedded in search and browse. The working team was unusually cross-functional: UX designers, customer experience PMs, data scientists, and a researcher. We partnered with Walmart's category specialists — in-house buyers who specialize in specific product verticals like home, fashion, and everyday living.

We mapped shopping journeys across three dimensions:

  • By occasion: Urgent problem-solving ("my remote died") vs. weekly routine ("restocking groceries") vs. planned research ("remodeling my living room")
  • By mental model: "Show me the cheapest" vs. "help me feel confident" vs. "I need to research options"
  • By product type: Low-consideration commodities (batteries) vs. high-consideration items (TVs, rugs) requiring multiple visits and more information
Framework Shopping journey framework — mapping occasions, mental models, and product types to different module strategies. This became the backbone of our design approach.

How I Built Alignment

I reframed success metrics away from pure efficiency toward customer guidance. The old metrics focused on search speed and filter usage. I advocated for metrics that measured whether customers actually found what they wanted: find-it score (search relevance on top results), add-to-cart rate, and NPS. This reframing — from "make search faster" to "make search easier" — aligned the team around outcomes rather than features.

03

Ideation & Decision Making

Design Explorations

We exhausted hundreds of ideas, tested and iterated on 50, and validated the strongest through co-creation studies (designing with the customer) and storyboard testing. From this process, I owned the design and delivery of two modules:

Top Picks — a flexible module aimed at reducing cognitive load. It pulls the top 4 choices from thousands of results into clear segments: top selling, best ratings, best deal, and budget-friendly (cheapest per unit). For low-consideration products like batteries, this gives customers an instant shortcut. For high-consideration products like Samsung TVs, the module translates technical specs (4K, 200Hz, UHD) into layman terms — "Great value" and "Budget friendly" — because telling a customer a TV has "200Hz" means nothing if they don't know what that is.

Top Picks · Batteries Low-consideration: Top Selling, Best Rated, Best Deal, Budget — instant shortcut for routine purchases
Top Picks · Samsung TVs High-consideration: Category specialist-curated picks translating specs like "4K 200Hz" into "Great Value" and "Budget Friendly"

Shop By — a module that progressively discloses contextual filters based on product attributes. For rugs (130K+ items), traditional text-based filters like "velvet" or "twill" don't mean anything to most customers. Instead, Shop By presents visual, image-based filters — shop by room, shop by style — that help customers narrow down through decisions they can actually see and understand.

Shop By · Rugs Visual image-based filters: "Shop by Room" with photographs instead of text labels like "Living Room" or "Bedroom"
Inline Filters Quick-select visual chips that reload results contextually without page refresh

The Trade-offs I Navigated

For Top Picks, I explored several visual treatments: colored backgrounds to distinguish segments (created an undesirable rainbow effect across pages), a 4-grid layout, and carousels. Color didn't work because it clashed at scale. I chose a fold-based layout, but learned during testing that placing all picks above the fold pushed organic results below — and we were giving up too much information per tile. We constrained each tile to 320px (optimized for iPhone screen width) and balanced horizontal and vertical scroll to accommodate the modules without burying organic results.

For Shop By, the trade-off was between traditional filter approaches (text lists, dropdowns) and visual/image-based filtering. Traditional filters were familiar but ineffective for categories like rug styles where the terminology is meaningless without visual context. I pushed for the image-based approach, backed by co-creation study findings where customers gravitated toward visual cues over text labels.

Iterations Design explorations — Top Picks with colored backgrounds (rainbow effect, rejected), 4-grid layout, carousel approach. Shop By text-based vs. image-based filter comparison. Co-creation study artifacts.
04

The Solution & Craft

The Core Solution

The modular search system worked as a layered experience. As customers scrolled through search results, contextual modules appeared inline — injected between organic product listings based on the query and category.

Top Picks appeared near the top of results, immediately surfacing 4 curated options segmented by what matters most for that category. For batteries: top selling, best rated, best deal, cheapest. For Samsung TVs: category specialist-curated picks with editorial descriptions that translated technical specs into human language.

Shop By surfaced further down the page, progressively disclosing filters at the point where a customer has scrolled past initial results and likely needs guidance. The contextual filters — shop by room for rugs, shop by size for TVs — automatically reloaded results, maintaining the customer's scroll position and flow.

Final Design The complete modular search experience — Top Picks, Shop By, and Inline Filters shown in context on a search results page. Modules inject contextually between organic listings.

Systems Thinking

The modular architecture was designed for extensibility. Each module was a self-contained unit that could be composed differently based on query context. This wasn't just two modules — it was the foundation for a modular search system that could scale to accommodate future modules as Walmart's search strategy evolved. I worked directly with frontend and backend engineering leads to ensure the framework supported dynamic injection, conditional rendering by category, and API-driven content from the category specialist teams.

Both modules were A/B tested against the existing search experience. Top Picks saw a significant increase in search relevance for the top 4 results, with a +0.66% conversion rate improvement — a substantial lift at Walmart's scale. Across queries where both modules were present, the average actions-to-cart dropped by ~6, meaning customers were finding what they wanted with meaningfully less effort.

The modular search foundation became the architectural pattern for Walmart's search evolution. The success of Top Picks and Shop By validated that contextual, category-aware modules outperform a one-size-fits-all search experience.

06

Reflections

What Went Well

The cross-functional team structure was a major strength. Having data scientists, category specialists, and researchers embedded in the design process meant every design decision could be validated against real product knowledge and customer data. The co-creation studies — literally designing with customers — gave us confidence that the modules addressed real shopping behaviors rather than assumed ones.

What I'd Do Differently

I would have invested more in personalization earlier. The modules were contextual to the query and category, but not yet personalized to the individual customer. A returning customer who always buys Duracell shouldn't see the same Top Picks layout as a first-time battery shopper. The modular architecture supported this, but we didn't get to implement it within my tenure.

What I Learned

Search is an ocean — swimming aimlessly gets you nowhere. This project taught me the importance of strategy: how to diverge and think broadly, but also how to distill hundreds of ideas into actionable, testable plans.

Working at Walmart's scale, with the talent and resources of a massive organization, I learned that even the most nebulous problems like "fix search and browse" become manageable when you collaborate, empower others to creatively ideate, and problem-solve together.

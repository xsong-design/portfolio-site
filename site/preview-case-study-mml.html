Marketing Message Liquidity

I designed the onboarding, budget handling, and post-creation reporting for integrating WhatsApp marketing messages into Ads Manager — helping advertisers understand and trust a new cross-channel optimization model that drove +20% ROAS in early tests.

Role Product Designer
Team 5 designers + PM + Eng
Timeline 2023 – H1 2024
Platform Ads Manager (Web)
Company Meta
Hero shot — the integrated campaign experience showing budget optimization across ads and WhatsApp marketing messages Insert visual

Meta needed to integrate WhatsApp marketing messages directly into Ads Manager campaigns so the system could optimize spend across ads and messages under a single budget. I owned the onboarding experience and budget handling — the two surfaces where advertisers' expectations clashed hardest with the product model. The alpha shipped to three businesses and showed +20% ROAS, +13% purchases, and -23% cost per add to cart vs. ads-only campaigns.

01

Context & Challenge

The Business Problem

WhatsApp marketing messages let businesses re-engage opted-in subscribers with targeted campaigns — a fundamentally different channel from ads. But Meta's Beta kept ads and marketing messages in completely separate workflows, forcing advertisers to run two campaigns with two budgets. This killed campaign performance and blocked adoption.

Integrating the two into a single campaign with optimized delivery could unlock $3.5B in incremental revenue by 2030. The target users were performance marketers at large advertisers with $20K+ monthly Ads Manager spend.

Before: the standalone WhatsApp Business messaging interface — separate budget, separate workflow Before

Two separate tools, two budgets, zero optimization between them

What Made This Hard

The project sat at the intersection of three organizations with competing priorities. Business Messaging wanted to maximize marketing message adoption and revenue. Ads Experience wanted to protect Ads Manager's streamlined workflow and avoid revenue regression. WhatsApp wanted to protect the consumer experience with higher friction guardrails.

My surfaces — onboarding and budget — were where these tensions collided hardest. Budget handling was the most politically charged design problem in the project: Business Messaging wanted optimized delivery on by default, Ads wanted minimal disruption to existing budget controls, and advertisers expected something entirely different from both.

02

Discovery & Strategy

How I Built Understanding

I was part of a 5-designer sprint that explored the end-to-end integrated experience. My focus areas were onboarding (how do advertisers activate and set up marketing messages?) and budget handling (how do advertisers understand and trust optimized delivery across ads and messages?).

Design sprint structure — the 5-designer team and their respective focus areas across the integrated experience Process

We ran prototype usability testing with 10 performance marketers across priority markets (Brazil, India, Mexico, Indonesia, US, UK). The findings on my surfaces were the most alarming:

  • All 10 participants raised significant concerns about optimized delivery to their subscribers
  • 9 out of 10 expected all subscribers to receive the campaign, spending budget on messages first — the exact opposite of how optimized delivery works
  • Advertisers mapped marketing messages to email campaigns (guaranteed delivery, explicit control) not ads (algorithmic optimization)

“With subscribers, they are my loyal customers so I expect significantly higher conversion and expect bulk of my budget (60-70%) to be assigned to WhatsApp. I will not be OK with the reverse because only I can determine the potential with WhatsApp for my business.”

Research participant — Performance marketer, India

This wasn't a UI problem — it was a conceptual gap. Advertisers fundamentally didn't expect a paid messaging product to provide optimized delivery. This insight reshaped how I approached both the onboarding flow and the budget controls.

Mental model diagram — how advertisers expected marketing messages to work (email model: guaranteed delivery, explicit budget control) vs. how the product actually worked (ads model: algorithmic optimization across channels) Research Insight

The conceptual gap: advertisers expected email-style control, the product delivered ads-style optimization

How I Built Alignment

I presented research findings to Monetization Design Leads alongside the broader team, framing the budget comprehension failure not as "the design didn't work" but as "we've uncovered a conceptual gap that needs both in-product and out-of-product mitigation." This opened the conversation to GTM education and sales enablement alongside product changes.

03

Ideation & Decisions

Design Explorations

The team explored 16 total options across the full experience. For my surfaces, the key explorations centered on two questions:

  • Onboarding: How much context do advertisers need before they create their first integrated campaign? And who is doing the setup — the same person creating campaigns, or someone else?
  • Budget: How do we communicate that delivery will be optimized across ads and messages when advertisers expect to control budget allocation explicitly?
Exploration grid — the 16 design options across the full integrated experience, with my focus areas (onboarding + budget) highlighted Explorations

16 explorations across 5 designers — my focus areas highlighted in orange

The research had shown that the person creating a campaign is often not the same person who manages account settings. This meant the creation flow couldn't assume the advertiser had seen the onboarding context. Every campaign needed to independently communicate that marketing messages were included and how budget would work.

The Trade-off I Navigated

For budget handling, I faced a direct conflict: the product model optimized delivery automatically (the system decides how to split spend for best ROI), but advertisers expected manual control (I decide how much goes to messages vs. ads). Giving full manual control would undermine the optimization that made integrated campaigns valuable. But hiding the allocation entirely would erode trust.

I designed for transparency without control — showing advertisers how budget was being allocated and what results it was producing, without giving them a lever to override the optimization. The estimated daily results card was extended to include "Messages Delivered" estimates, giving advertisers a preview of the split before they hit publish.

04

Solution & Craft

Onboarding

I designed a proactive activation flow available from multiple entry points in Ads Manager. Only businesses that explicitly activated marketing messages at the account level would see any changes in their creation flow. After activation, setting up reply handling and uploading subscribers were required gates before a campaign could send messages. For high-volume senders, I designed subscriber guardrails — rate limits on initial campaigns with a formal verification path for businesses that needed to send at scale.

Onboarding flow — the step-by-step activation experience: account-level opt-in → reply handling setup → subscriber upload → campaign creation enabled Flow

Progressive disclosure: each gate unlocked the next step of the integration

Budget Handling

The estimated daily results card was extended with a "Messages Delivered" estimate alongside existing reach and impression projections. This gave advertisers a concrete expectation of how many subscribers would receive messages — directly addressing the research finding that 9 out of 10 expected all subscribers to be messaged. By showing the estimate upfront, advertisers could calibrate expectations before publishing.

Budget handling UI — the extended estimated daily results card showing Messages Delivered alongside reach and impressions, with the delivery split visualization UI Detail

Post-Creation Reporting

I designed the integrated campaigns table to surface marketing messages as a line item alongside ad placements in the existing breakdowns experience. Advertisers could compare ROI across ads and messages within the same view. "Delivered Messages" was introduced as a new metric in performance presets — surfaced through lightweight onboarding after an advertiser's first integrated campaign.

The final creation flow shipped as a hybrid — both the Audience card and Placements card included marketing message controls, giving advertisers two touchpoints to understand and manage whether their campaign included messages.

Systems Thinking

The reporting patterns I designed — integrating a non-ad delivery channel as a comparable line item in the campaigns table — established a framework for how future messaging products could surface alongside ads in performance reporting. The activation flow pattern (account-level opt-in → readiness gates → creation integration) was designed to be reusable for future non-ad capabilities in Ads Manager.

06

Reflections

What Went Well

The design sprint format — 5 designers exploring the full experience end-to-end before splitting into focused working teams — was the right call for a project of this scope. It gave everyone shared context on the integrated experience before we went deep on individual surfaces. I built relationships with designers across Ads Experience that carried through the entire project.

What I'd Do Differently

I would have pushed to involve the GTM and sales team earlier in the budget handling design. Much of the comprehension gap around optimized delivery couldn't be fully solved in-product — advertisers needed education from their Meta reps to recalibrate expectations from email-style guaranteed delivery to algorithmic optimization. Having that education strategy in place earlier would have let me make bolder design choices in the creation flow.

What I Learned

Working this broadly — across Business Messaging, Ads, and WhatsApp — and then later partnering closely with Ads counterparts on build-ready specs was one of the best professional experiences I've had. The scale of the collaboration taught me that the hardest design problems aren't about pixels. They're about aligning competing organizational incentives around what's actually best for the user. The budget handling design only worked because I understood what all three orgs needed, not just my own.
